# Step 1: Theme (the Direction)

Decides what the thing should feel like and where its look comes from, before any colour or font
is chosen. It takes the brief and hands every later step a short **Direction block** to cite.
Skipping this step is the root cause of slop: with nothing specific to draw from, every later
choice falls back to the most common one.

## Method

1. **Inventory the subject.** Answer in plain nouns and verbs, not adjectives:
   - what it is and what it does (the verbs: book, weigh, dye, ship, teach);
   - who uses it, where, and in what state (rushed, idle, on a phone in sunlight, at a desk);
   - its era and place (a 1970s ferry line, a Pekalongan dye house, a Jakarta clinic);
   - what it's made of or surrounded by: materials, tools, signage, packaging, uniforms;
   - what it competes with, so you know what to look different from.
2. **Pick three adjectives, each with the neighbour it rejects:** "precise, not clinical", "warm,
   not cute", "plain-spoken, not blunt". The "X, not Y" form stops an adjective drifting into its
   cliché (Mailchimp's voice guide uses it).
   - **Banned as direction words:** modern, clean, minimal, sleek, innovative, cutting-edge,
     premium, intuitive, futuristic. They fit every brand, so they decide nothing.
3. **Place the personality sliders** (GV Brand Sprint):
   - elite ↔ mass appeal
   - serious ↔ playful
   - conventional ↔ rebel
   - friend ↔ authority
   - classic ↔ young

   Sliders describe the brand's *voice*: they shape the copy, the logo and the signature. They
   don't reach the reading surfaces. A "rebel, playful" brand still gets a calm ground, a legible
   body face and an aligned grid ([comfort.md](comfort.md)). Put a slider in the middle when the
   brand really is in the middle.
4. **List 5–8 material-world nouns.** These are the physical things the look borrows from: "indigo
   vat, wax resist, cotton cambric, copper stamp, drying rack". Colour hues, texture, pattern, icon
   style and illustration are later drawn from this list. On reading surfaces a noun shows only as
   a hue tint; at full strength it belongs to the signature.
5. **Name one era, place or culture reference** that is specific enough to argue with ("Dutch
   railway signage, 1960s", "Javanese wayang shadow cut-outs", "a pharmacy label from a Bandung
   apotek"). One reference, used with judgement, not a pastiche. It may show in the logo, the
   display face, one illustration or pattern; never in body text, forms or tables.
6. **Write 2–3 design principles** that settle real trade-offs, e.g. "the timetable is the hero;
   decoration never pushes it below the fold". A principle that nobody could disagree with is
   not a principle.
7. **Choose the intensity and name the signature.** Calm, Balanced or Bold
   ([comfort.md → Intensity](comfort.md#intensity)); Calm unless the brief or the subject is loud,
   and ask one question if unsure. Then name the signature: the logo, plus one item at Calm (up to
   two at Balanced or Bold) from a display face, a bold use of the accent, imagery or illustration,
   one layout gesture, one pattern. Everything else stays on the comfort floor.
8. **For a new brand, sketch two or three named directions** and compare them before committing
   (Hike One's design-direction method: name and describe each in words, then a moodboard, then a
   style tile). Pick one, and say what the losing ones taught you.
9. **Write the Direction block** and keep it at the top of the design brief:

```
Direction
  Subject:     what it is, who uses it, where, the one job of this screen/brand
  Adjectives:  X, not Y; X, not Y; X, not Y
  Sliders:     serious ●───○ playful … (only the ones that matter; they set the voice)
  Material:    5–8 nouns from the subject's world
  Reference:   one era / place / culture reference
  Principles:  2–3 trade-off rules
  Intensity:   Calm / Balanced / Bold, and why
  Signature:   the logo + the 1–2 items where the subject shows at full strength
```

Every later decision cites one line of this block: "olive #4E5A2B, from the drying-rack bamboo
(Material)", "slab serif for the display lines, from the 1960s railway reference (Reference)".

### Optional: look at Figma Community, when a browser is available

Search https://www.figma.com/community/ for the subject's own category ("hotel", "law firm",
"bakery", "logistics", "batik"), never "AI SaaS" or "startup landing page", which are where the
generic templates cluster. Open 3–5 files.
- **Note what makes each one specific:** the photo, the material, a type choice tied to the
  place.
- **Never copy** a layout, palette or mark.
- **Moodboard and style-tile files** (Mixpanel's Moodboard Kit, "Style Tile Examples", the
  8-category direction board) are useful as structure, not as content.

## Slop to avoid

- **Starting with a palette or a font.** The look then comes from whatever came first, not from
  the subject.
- **Direction words that fit every brand** (the list in step 2).
- **"Tech" as a theme.** "It's software" is not a direction; what the software is *about* is.
- **Borrowing another product's look** ("make it like Linear/Stripe/Vercel"), unless the brief
  literally asks for it. Even then, take the principle, not the palette.
- **A theme reference that's really a template category** ("dashboard", "SaaS", "portfolio").
- **Turning every axis up at once.** Extreme sliders, a material colour on the page, an era face
  for body text and a tension grid together make a design feel forced and tiring. Spend the
  Direction in the signature; see [comfort.md](comfort.md).

## Checks

- Could the Direction block describe a competitor, or a different product entirely? If yes, it's
  too vague; make the nouns more concrete.
- Does every adjective have a "not Y"?
- Can you point at a real-world object for each material noun?
- Is the signature named, and within the intensity's budget?
- Does anything from the Direction reach the reading surfaces as more than a tint or the voice?
